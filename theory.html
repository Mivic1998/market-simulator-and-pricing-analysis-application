Overview

This simulator is designed to help users understand how markets work by allowing them to experiment with supply, demand, taxation, revenue, elasticity, and welfare outcomes in real time.

At the centre of the simulator are two graphs. The main market graph shows the interaction between buyers and sellers using demand and supply curves. In demand mode, a second graph shows total revenue, which helps explain how pricing decisions affect the amount of money received by firms.

As the user adjusts the controls, the simulator recalculates the market outcome automatically. This means the curves move, the equilibrium point changes, the shaded welfare areas update, the metrics refresh, and the written insights explain what is happening.

The simulator contains two main modes:

  • Demand Mode, which focuses on pricing, revenue and associated welfare outcomes.
  • Supply Mode, which focuses on taxation and resulting market distortion and deadweight loss.

Within each mode, users can switch between three different demand models:

  • Linear Demand
  • Non-Linear Demand
  • Income-Based Demand

The purpose of the simulator is not to predict one specific real-world market. Instead, it provides a visual demonstration of the basic economic principles that help explain how competitive markets behave.

Understanding Supply and Demand

Every market consists of buyers and sellers.

Buyers create demand. Demand measures how much of a product consumers are willing and able to purchase at different prices.

Sellers create supply. Supply measures how much of a product producers are willing and able to sell at different prices.

The simulator displays these relationships using two curves:

  • The demand curve, which usually slopes downward.
  • The supply curve, which usually slopes upward.

Why Demand Slopes Downward

A useful way to understand demand is through consumer valuation. Different consumers place different values on a product. Some people value a product highly and are willing to pay a high price. Others only value it enough to buy it when the price is low.

As the price falls, more consumers decide that the product is worth buying. This is why quantity demanded usually increases when price decreases.

Why Supply Slopes Upward

Supply can be understood through producer costs. Some firms can produce cheaply and are willing to sell even at low prices. Other firms face higher costs and only enter the market when the price is high enough to cover those costs.

As the price rises, production becomes more profitable, so more firms are willing to sell and existing firms may produce more.

These two forces together (demand and supply) determine the outcome of the market.

Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Qd = Qs

On the main graph, this is the point where the supply and demand curves intersect.

At equilibrium:

  • Every unit produced can be sold.
  • Every consumer willing to pay the market price can buy.
  • There is no excess demand.
  • There is no excess supply.

The simulator automatically calculates this point and displays the corresponding price and quantity in the metrics panel. In a competitive market, market forces will always push towards this equilibrium outcome. The following two sections explain how a competitive market is self-correcting when prices deviate from equilibrium.

What Happens Above Equilibrium?

If the market price is above equilibrium, firms want to sell more than consumers want to buy. This creates excess supply, meaning producers have unsold goods.

To avoid being left with stock they cannot sell, firms reduce prices. As prices fall, demand increases and supply decreases until the market moves back towards equilibrium.

What Happens Below Equilibrium?

If the market price is below equilibrium, consumers want to buy more than firms are willing to sell. This creates excess demand, or a shortage.

Consumers compete for the limited supply available, which puts upward pressure on prices. As prices rise, demand falls and supply increases until the market returns to equilibrium.

Demand Curves

This section outlines the different types of demand curves that users can explore in the simulator and their parameters.

Linear Demand

The linear demand curve follows the equation:

Q = a - bP

This equation describes how the quantity demanded changes as price changes.

  • a represents the quantity/horizontal intercept of the demand curve and controls the overall size of the market.
  • b determines how strongly quantity demanded responds to changes in price (price sensitivity).

The parameter a determines the maximum quantity consumers would purchase if the good were available at a price of zero.

Increasing a shifts the demand curve outward, increasing demand at every price level. This can represent a larger market, a greater number of consumers, or stronger overall demand for the product.

The parameter b determines how quickly demand falls as price increases.

Increasing b makes consumers more responsive to price changes. As a result, quantity demanded decreases more rapidly as price rises.

A lower value of b results in consumers being less responsive to price changes, producing a more inelastic demand curve.

Because the relationship between price and quantity is represented by a straight line, the linear model is relatively easy to analyse and provides a useful starting point for understanding market behaviour.

The simulator uses this model to demonstrate how changes in market size and consumer responsiveness affect equilibrium outcomes, revenue, elasticity, and welfare.

Non-Linear Demand

The non-linear demand model is designed to represent markets where consumer behaviour cannot be accurately described by a straight line.

In the linear demand model, every increase in price reduces demand by the same amount. While this makes the mathematics simple, real markets often behave differently. Consumers may react very strongly to price changes in some situations and much less strongly in others.

To capture this behaviour, the simulator includes an exponential demand model:

Q = ae-bP

Unlike the linear model, this produces a curved demand function rather than a straight line.

The curve falls rapidly at some prices and more gradually at others, allowing consumer responsiveness to vary across different parts of the market.

  • a controls the overall size of the market and represents the quantity/horizontal intercept of the demand curve.
  • b controls how quickly demand falls as prices increase.

Increasing a shifts the demand curve outward, increasing demand at every price level. This can represent a larger market, greater consumer interest, or stronger overall demand for the product.

Increasing b makes consumers more sensitive to price changes. Demand falls more rapidly as price increases, resulting in a steeper curve and lower quantities demanded at higher prices.

One important consequence of the non-linear model is that the shape of the demand curve changes across different price levels.

In some parts of the curve, quantity demanded falls very quickly as price rises. In other parts of the curve, quantity demanded changes more gradually.

This behaviour can often provide a more realistic representation of consumer decision-making than a simple straight-line demand curve.

The non-linear demand model can therefore produce different equilibrium outcomes from the linear model, even when the overall size of the market appears similar.

It also changes the shape of the welfare areas displayed on the graph. Because the demand curve is curved rather than straight, consumer surplus is no longer represented by a simple triangle. The simulator therefore calculates this area using integration rather than basic geometric formulas as in the linear case.

Comparing the non-linear and linear demand models helps demonstrate how different assumptions about consumer behaviour can lead to different market outcomes.

Income-Based Demand

The income-based demand model links consumer demand directly to income.

The model is derived from a Cobb-Douglas utility function:

U(x,y) = xky1-k

In this function, x represents consumption of the good being analysed in the simulator, while yis a composite good which represents consumption of all other goods. Together, they reflect how consumers allocate their income between this good and everything else they could purchase subject to a budget constraint.

When the consumer maximises his utility, the resulting demand function is:

x = kI/P

where k and I are constants that can be adjusted by the user. The demand curve is hyperbolic, meaning it becomes flatter as quantity increases.

  • x represents consumption of the good in the simulator (measured on the horizontal axis on the main graph).
  • I represents consumer income (a constant to be adjusted by the user).
  • k represents the proportion of income consumers choose to spend on the good (a constant to be adjusted by the user).
  • P represents price (measured on the vertical axis on the main graph).

This means demand increases as income rises and decreases as price rises.

Supply Curves

While demand describes the behaviour of consumers, supply describes the behaviour of producers.

A supply curve shows the quantity of a good that firms are willing and able to sell at different prices.

In the simulator, the supply curve is represented by a linear supply function:

Q = c + dP

  • c represents the quantity/horizontal intercept of the supply curve.
  • d determines how strongly quantity supplied responds to changes in price (supply responsiveness).

The supply curve normally slopes upward because higher prices make production more profitable. As prices rise, more firms are willing to enter the market and existing firms are willing to produce more output.

Increasing c shifts the supply curve to the right, increasing the quantity supplied at every price level.

Increasing d makes supply more responsive to price changes, resulting in a flatter supply curve.

Changes in the supply curve affect the market equilibrium. An increase in supply generally causes the equilibrium price to fall and the equilibrium quantity to rise, while a decrease in supply tends to raise prices and reduce output.

Alternative Curve Representation

In standard economic theory, demand and supply curves are often written in inverse form, where price is expressed as a function of quantity (P = f(Q)).

In this form:

  • The demand curve shows the maximum price consumers are willing to pay for each unit.
  • The supply curve shows the minimum price producers are willing to accept for each unit.

However, in this simulator, both demand and supply are written as functions of price:

Q = a − bP

Q = c + dP

This formulation is used because it simplifies computation within the model. Many of the simulator’s interactive features—such as calculating equilibrium, revenue, taxation effects, and dynamically updating graphs—require repeatedly evaluating quantities at different prices. Writing the equations in this form allows these calculations to be carried out more directly and efficiently.

Despite this, the standard economic interpretation can still be recovered by rearranging the equations.

Demand: P = (a − Q) / b

Supply: P = (Q − c) / d

In these rearranged forms, the demand curve represents willingness to pay and the supply curve represents the minimum cost of production. These interpretations are used when analysing concepts such as consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total welfare.

This means that although the simulator uses Q = f(P) for modelling and computation, it remains fully consistent with standard economic theory.

Consumer and Producer Surplus

Consumer Surplus

Consumer surplus measures the benefit consumers receive from participating in the market.

Imagine a consumer who would have been willing to pay £20 for a product but only has to pay the market price of £10. That consumer receives a benefit of £10 because the product is worth more to them than the price they actually pay.

Many consumers experience similar gains. The demand curve represents the maximum price consumers are willing to pay for each quantity, so (under certain simplifying assumptions), by summing over all bought units, the area between the demand curve and the market price gives us consumer surplus. This is the total of these benefits across all consumers.

In the simulator, consumer surplus is therefore represented by the blue shaded area between the demand curve and the market price.

Producer Surplus

Producer surplus measures the benefit producers receive from participating in the market.

Imagine a producer who would have been willing to sell a product for £5 but receives the market price of £10. That producer receives a benefit of £5 because they receive more than the minimum amount needed to make the sale worthwhile.

Many producers experience similar gains. The supply curve represents the minimum price at which producers are willing to sell each quantity, so (under certain simplifying assumptions), by summing over all sold units, the area between the supply curve and the market price gives us producer surplus. This is the total of these benefits across all producers.

In the simulator, producer surplus is therefore represented by the green shaded area between the supply curve and the market price.

Total Welfare

Consumer surplus and producer surplus together form total welfare. Welfare represents the total gains created by voluntary trade in a market.

Total welfare is maximised at the competitive equilibrium because this is the point at which every mutually beneficial trade takes place.

At the equilibrium quantity, every consumer who values the good more than it costs producers to supply it is able to buy the good, and every producer who can profitably supply the good is able to sell it.

This means that the total gains from trade are as large as possible.

However, firms do not always choose to operate at the competitive equilibrium quantity.

In Demand Mode, the simulator also highlights the quantity that maximises total revenue.

Revenue maximisation often involves reducing output below the competitive equilibrium quantity and charging a higher price.

While this can increase the firm's revenue, it causes some mutually beneficial trades to disappear.

Consumers who would have been willing to purchase the good at the equilibrium price are no longer able or willing to buy it at the higher price.

As a result, some consumer surplus is lost.

Producers may gain from the higher price received on remaining sales, but the reduction in output means that some gains from trade disappear entirely.

This creates a welfare loss because units that would have generated value for both buyers and sellers are no longer traded.

In Demand Mode, this welfare loss (the sum of lost consumer and producer surplus due to output reduction) is represented by the red triangular shaded area between the equilibrium quantity and the revenue-maximising quantity as the sliders are adjusted.

The simulator allows users to compare the equilibrium quantity with the revenue-maximising quantity, helping illustrate the trade-off between maximising market welfare and maximising firm revenue.

Total Revenue

In Demand Mode, the simulator displays a second graph showing total revenue. While the main graph shows the relationship between price and quantity, the revenue graph shows the total income generated from sales at different points along the demand curve.

TR = P × Q

Total revenue is calculated by multiplying the price of the good by the quantity sold.

The key idea is that the revenue graph is not separate from the demand curve. It is generated directly from the demand curve.

Every point on the demand curve gives one possible combination of price and quantity. For each point:

  1. The demand curve determines the price consumers are willing to pay at that quantity.
  2. The simulator calculates revenue using Price × Quantity.
  3. That revenue value is plotted on the revenue graph.

This means that when the demand curve changes, the revenue curve also changes.

For example, a high price may generate little revenue if very few units are sold. A low price may also generate little revenue if each unit earns very little. The highest revenue usually occurs somewhere between these extremes.

For linear and non-linear demand, the simulator highlights the revenue-maximising point on the revenue graph. This shows the price and quantity combination that produces the greatest total revenue.

The revenue graph also has a hover system, which allows users to explore how revenue changes at different output levels. Since revenue is constant across all output levels in the income-demand case, this feature is omitted in this scenario to avoid confusion.

Taxation, Supply and Deadweight Loss

Earlier, we saw that total welfare is maximised at the competitive equilibrium, where every mutually beneficial trade takes place. We also saw that restricting output below the equilibrium level can create a welfare loss because some consumers and producers who would have benefited from trading are no longer able to do so.

In Demand Mode, this can occur when a firm restricts output in order to increase revenue. In Supply Mode, the simulator focuses on another common cause of welfare loss: taxation.

In Supply Mode, the simulator introduces a per-unit tax on producers.

A tax increases the cost of selling each unit of a good. Even if firms continue producing in exactly the same way as before, they must now pay an additional amount to the government every time they make a sale.

This means that producers effectively face higher costs.

To remain profitable, firms attempt to pass some or all of this additional cost onto consumers through higher prices.

The simulator models this by shifting the supply curve upward:

P = (Q - c) / d + t

where t represents the tax per unit.

When the tax is introduced:

  • Consumers pay a higher price.
  • Producers receive a lower effective price after tax.
  • Fewer units are bought and sold.
  • Market output falls below the competitive equilibrium level.

The reduction in output is important because some mutually beneficial trades no longer take place. There are consumers who value the good more than it costs producers to supply it, but the tax creates a gap between the price consumers pay and the amount producers receive which forces consumers who would have traded to abstain.

As a result, both consumer surplus and producer surplus decrease.

Part of this lost surplus is transferred to the government as tax revenue.

However, not all of the lost surplus is transferred. Some of the value that would have been created by trade disappears entirely.

This lost value is called deadweight loss.

Deadweight loss represents the welfare that would have been generated by trades that no longer occur because of the tax, or the sum of lost consumer and producer surplus resulting from the fall in output caused by the tax.

In the simulator:
  • The orange rectangular area represents tax revenue collected by the government (calculated as the per unit tax multiplied by the quantity sold at the new output level).
  • The red triangular area represents deadweight loss.
  • The reduction in quantity traded shows that fewer mutually beneficial trades take place.
  • The larger the tax, the greater the distortion to the market.

By adjusting the tax slider, users can observe the trade-off between raising government revenue and maintaining market efficiency. As taxes increase, tax revenue initially rises, but output falls and deadweight loss generally becomes larger.

Elasticity

Not all consumers respond to price changes in the same way. Some products experience large changes in demand when prices change, while others experience very little change.

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. It is calculated using percentage changes rather than absolute changes:

PED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)

Because elasticity is based on percentages, it depends on the starting values of price and quantity. This is important because it means elasticity is not constant along a demand curve, even when the curve is a straight line.

  • Elastic demand: quantity demanded changes proportionally more than price (PED > 1).
  • Inelastic demand: quantity demanded changes proportionally less than price (PED < 1).
  • Unit elastic: quantity demanded and price change by the same proportion (PED = 1).

Why Elasticity Varies Along a Linear Demand Curve

A linear demand curve has a constant slope, meaning each price change leads to the same absolute change in quantity. However, elasticity depends on percentage changes, not absolute changes.

At different points on the curve, the same change in price represents a different percentage change, and the same change in quantity represents a different percentage change. This causes elasticity to vary.

  • At high prices and low quantities (top of the curve): a small price change is a small percentage change in price, but the resulting change in quantity is large in percentage terms. Demand is therefore elastic.
  • At the midpoint: percentage changes in price and quantity are equal. Demand is unit elastic.
  • At low prices and high quantities (bottom of the curve): the same price change is large in percentage terms, but the change in quantity is small in percentage terms. Demand is therefore inelastic.
Key insight: Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve because it is calculated using percentages. Even though the slope is constant, percentage changes in price and quantity are not.

The simulator calculates elasticity dynamically when users hover over the demand curve, allowing this variation to be explored visually across different parts of the market. Since elasticity is constant across all output levels in the income-demand case, this feature is omitted in this scenario to avoid confusion.

How to Use the Simulator

  1. Select either Demand Mode or Supply Mode.
  2. Choose a demand model.
  3. Adjust parameters using the sliders or manual inputs.
  4. Observe how the curves, equilibrium point, shaded areas, and metrics change.
  5. Use the hover features to explore elasticity and revenue interactively.
  6. Review the insights panel to understand the meaning of the current market outcome.
  7. Experiment with presets to compare different market scenarios quickly.

The simulator is designed as an interactive economics laboratory. Instead of only reading definitions or looking at static diagrams, users can immediately see how markets respond when assumptions and conditions change.
